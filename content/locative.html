Miejscownik
the case that always has a preposition

The locative answers o kim? o czym? w czym? gdzie? — “about whom? about what? where?”. It is the only Polish case that cannot stand alone: it is always governed by one of five prepositions — na, o, po, przy, w. That single fact makes it the easiest case to recognise and, because of the sound shifts it triggers, one of the harder ones to spell. Every rule and every form on this page was checked against the PWN language clinic and against dictionary declension tables.

01

What the locative is

The locative — miejscownik, in Latin locativus, which is why Polish grammars abbreviate it Ms. — is the sixth of the seven Polish cases. In the traditional school order it comes after the instrumental: mianownik, dopełniacz, celownik, biernik, narzędnik, miejscownik, wołacz.

Its defining property is that it is never bare. Every noun, adjective, pronoun and numeral in the locative stands behind one of exactly five prepositions:

na o po przy w (and its variant we)

That is the whole list. If you see a noun after any other preposition — do, z, przez, dla, bez, nad, pod, przed, za, między, ku, od, sprzed — it is not in the locative, whatever it looks like. This is why the locative is the easiest case to spot in a real sentence: you never have to guess, you look at the word in front of it.

Note

The same preposition can take a different case with a different meaning, and that difference is the heart of this case. na takes the locative for a place where (na poczcie — at the post office) and the accusative for a place to which (na pocztę — to the post office). o takes the locative when you talk about something (o filmie) and the accusative when you ask for something (o film). The preposition alone does not decide; the meaning does. Section 9 takes each one apart.

Where the name comes from — and why it is misleading

“Locative” suggests location, and location is indeed its most common job. But the name is a poor description of the whole case. The locative also marks the object of dozens of everyday verbs that have nothing to do with place — mówić o kimś (to talk about someone), polegać na kimś (to rely on someone), obstawać przy czymś (to insist on something) — and it can express time and even manner. Polish grammars therefore call it by its shape, not its function: the case that appears in a certain set of prepositional phrases.

The classification of its functions below follows Hanna Jadacka’s entry “Miejscownik” in the PWN Poradnia Językowa (8 April 2025).

The three exceptions to -ach

Three country names take -ech instead: w Niemczech (in Germany), na Węgrzech (in Hungary), we Włoszech (in Italy). Every other country is regular: w Czechach, w Indiach, w Chinach, we Francji, w Polsce, w Anglii. This is the single most quoted exception in Polish grammar, so learn the three together as a chant.

The -e group

Masculine and neuter hard stems (not k, g, ch, l), and feminine hard stems (not l). The ending -e softens the last consonant of the stem, which is where all the spelling difficulty lives: miasto → mieście, droga → drodze.

The -u group

Masculine and neuter stems that are already soft or hardened, plus every stem in k, g, ch, l. No softening happens — the stem is already where it needs to be: koń → koniu, bank → banku, hotel → hotelu.

The -i / -y split

Feminine only. Soft stems and l take -i; hardened stems (c, cz, dz, dż, rz, sz, ż) take -y. The choice is not free and not a matter of taste: tradycji but pracy, soli but nocy.

03

What the locative is used for

The case has four jobs, in sharply falling order of frequency. Recognising which one you are looking at tells you which preposition to expect, and the preposition is usually the only cue you need.

1. The object of a prepositional verb — dopełnienie dalsze

This is the largest group by far, and the one learners underestimate. Polish has a large family of verbs and expressions that require a fixed preposition plus the locative. You do not choose the case; the verb does.

PatternVerbsExample
o + Ms. mówić, opowiadać, przypominać, rozmawiać, wspominać, myśleć, marzyć, wiedzieć, zapomnieć Rozmawiamy o pracy.
na + Ms. polegać, wzorować się, koncentrować się, opierać się, siedzieć, skupiać się, zasadzać się, znać się Polegam na tobie.
przy + Ms. obstawać, upierać się, stać, mieszkać (rare), pracować Upieram się przy swoim zdaniu.
po + Ms. biegać, chodzić, deptać, jeździć, spacerować, stąpać Spacerujemy po parku.
Note

Two of these deserve a warning. Myśleć o czymś is locative (Myślę o tobie), but myśleć, że… takes a whole clause. And znać się na czymś (to know about something, to be good at it) is locative, while plain znać kogoś is accusative: Znam się na komputerach but Znam tego człowieka.

2. Place where — okolicznik miejsca

The function the case is named after, and the one you will meet in your first week of Polish: mieszkać w małym miasteczku, przebywać w więzieniu, przechowywać biżuterię w sejfie, zagubić się w kniei, zasiadać w radzie nadzorczej.

The prepositions here are w (inside, in) and na (on, at, in — for surfaces, open spaces and a fixed list of places), plus przy (by, next to) and po (around, over an area).

3. Time — okolicznik czasu — much rarer than you would think

Polish has no general “at” for clock time in the locative. The genuinely locative time expressions are a short, fixed list, all of them idiomatic:

ExpressionMeaning
wrócić o zmrokuto come back at dusk
zapalić po wyjściuto light up after leaving
załatwić przy okazjito sort out while at it, on occasion
we dnie i w nocyby day and by night (a fixed phrase — see section 6)

But do not conclude that the locative is the case of time — it is not. Days of the week take the accusative after w: w poniedziałek, we wtorek (never *w poniedziałku). Clock time uses o plus the locative of an ordinal, which is a numeral rather than a noun: o trzeciej, o wpół do piątej. Months are locative and perfectly regular — w styczniu, w lutym, w marcu, w kwietniu, w maju, w czerwcu, w lipcu, w sierpniu, we wrześniu, w październiku, w listopadzie, w grudniu — and so is the word for year in w 2026 roku. What matters is this: when a time expression happens to be locative, it is because a noun is standing behind w, not because time as such belongs to this case.

4. Manner and condition — a handful of idioms

Genuinely sporadic, and best learned as phrases rather than as rules:

ExpressionMeaningNote
przeżyć o chlebie i wodzieto live on bread and watermanner / means
zwyciężyć przy odrobinie szczęściato win with a bit of luckcondition
zaryzykować w sprzyjających okolicznościachto take a risk in favourable circumstancescondition
How to use this section

Do not memorise the four functions as a taxonomy. Use them as a diagnostic. If you can ask o kim? o czym?, the verb is governing the locative — learn the verb. If you can ask gdzie?, you are in the place function — learn the w/na contrast. Everything else is a phrase to collect.

04

Singular endings

Four endings, and the choice between them is decided by two things and two things only: the gender of the noun and the final consonant of its stem. Not the meaning, not the nominative ending. Get those two facts and the rule is deterministic.

Vocabulary for the rule

Hard consonants: p b t d f w s z m n r and k g ch (these three are velar, and they behave specially). Soft: ś ź ć dź ń j l. Hardened (historically soft, now hard): c cz dz dż rz sz ż. The four endings below are driven by exactly these three groups.

Masculine and neuter: -e or -u

The rule has one shape with one carve-out. A masculine or neuter stem ending in a hard consonant takes -e — unless that consonant is k, g, ch or l. A stem ending in a soft or hardened consonant, or in k, g, ch, l, takes -u.

Stem ends inEndingWhyExamples
-p -b -t -d -f -w -s -z -m -n -r -e hard trafie (traf) · arabie (arab) · buncie (bunt) · śladzie (ślad) · tronie (tron) · sznurze (sznur)
-k -g -ch -u velar — the carve-out banku · języku · pociągu · dachu · piachu · roku · mleku
-l -u the carve-out hotelu · kowalu · nauczycielu · polu · fotelu
-c -cz -dz -dż -rz -sz -ż -u hardened ojcu · graczu · mistrzu · lekarzu · haszyszu · łożu
-ś -ź -ć -dź -ń -j -u soft liściu (liść) · koniu (koń) · cieniu (cień) · gościu (gość) · kraju (kraj) · pokoju (pokój)

The -e ending is not a passive suffix. It softens whatever consonant it lands on, and that is why the spelling changes on the way in. This is the single most common source of mistakes in the whole case, so section 7 gives it a table of its own. For now, the pattern:

miasto → mieście  ·  las → lesie  ·  wiatr → wietrze  ·  kocioł → kotle  ·  cokół → cokole
Worked examples

standard ends in d, hard → standardzie. głaz ends in z, hard → głazie. malkontent ends in t, hard → malkontencie. mieszczanin ends in n, hard → mieszczaninie. piętno → piętnie. ciasto → cieście.

No “-ek exception” exists

Learners often invent a rule that words ending in -ek behave differently. They do not. What matters is the consonant that actually closes the stem after any fleeting vowel has done its work: miasteczko → miasteczku (k), mleko → mleku (k), bank → banku (k). So the -u rule applies for one reason and one reason only — the stem ends in k. There is nothing extra to memorise.

Feminine: -e, -i or -y

Feminine nouns split three ways, and this is where most learners go wrong, because the popular shortcut “soft → -i” is only half the truth. The correct rule:

Stem ends inEndingExamples
a hard consonant, except l -e (+ softening) słocie (słota) · lamie (lama) · stodole (stodoła) · urodzie (uroda) · metaforze (metafora) · kolebie (koleba) · tapioce (tapioka) · altanie (altana) · szkole · kobiecie · drodze · poczcie
a soft consonant, or l -i stali (stal) · kąpieli (kąpiel) · nosogardzieli · tradycji · posesji · monarchini · miłości · gałęzi · armii · ziemi · kuchni · jesieni
a hardened consonant (c, cz, dz, dż, rz, sz, ż) -y cieczy · niszy · łupieży · macierzy · macy · miedzy · pracy · ulicy · nocy · plaży · twarzy
The rule most courses get wrong

A feminine stem in c, cz, dz, dż, rz, sz, ż takes -y, not -i. So: praca → pracy, ulica → ulicy, noc → nocy, plaża → plaży, twarz → twarzy, miedza → miedzy. These consonants are historically soft but are now pronounced hard, and the spelling follows the pronunciation. If you have been taught “soft stem → -i” as a blanket rule, this is the correction: -i is for genuinely soft consonants and l; hardened ones take -y.

Why l is on the soft side for feminine nouns

Polish l is a peculiar consonant: hard in pronunciation, but it behaves as soft in the feminine locative. So sól → soli, stal → stali, kąpiel → kąpieli, nosogardziel → nosogardzieli. But in masculine and neuter nouns, l takes -u: hotel → hotelu, kowal → kowalu, pole → polu. Same consonant, opposite behaviour, because the gender groups differ. Keep those two facts in separate boxes in your head.

Feminine nouns whose stem ends in a vowel letter

Nouns in -ia, -ja, -ość, -ść and the like are all soft stems and take -i, which combines with the preceding vowel into a single sound and a single spelling. This is where the double letters come from:

The -ii vs -i trap

Both historii and ziemi are correct, and the difference is not arbitrary — but it is also not worth deriving from a rule in the middle of a sentence. The safe method: if the nominative ends in -ia and the i is preceded by a consonant that stays, you normally get -ii (Anglia → Anglii, Hiszpania → Hiszpanii, Armenia → Armenii, linia → linii); when the -ia is a softening of a softer stem, you get a single -i (ziemia → ziemi, kuchnia → kuchni, studnia → studni). When in doubt, check the dictionary — this is a spelling question, not a grammar one, and native speakers check it too.

05

Plural: one ending, three exceptions

The plural is the easy part. Every noun of every gender takes -ach in the locative plural. There is no gender split, no softening, no choice.

butach · krowach · bagnach · domach · miastach · kotach · psach · kobietach · książkach · oknach · dzieciach

And then there are three words. Three country names take -ech instead of -ach:

CountryLocative pluralFull phrase
NiemcyNiemczechw Niemczech
WęgryWęgrzechna Węgrzech
WłochyWłoszechwe Włoszech

It is worth seeing how narrowly the exception is drawn. Every other plural country name is completely regular:

Regular -achLocative
Czechyw Czechach
Indiew Indiach
Chinyw Chinach
Katowicew Katowicach
Tatryw Tatrach
Mazuryna Mazurach
Kielcew Kielcach
drzwiw drzwiach
A mnemonic that actually works

The three exceptions are all plurals of historically dual or tribal names — they decline as if their stem ended in a soft consonant, which is exactly the environment where -ach became -ech. Rather than remember the history, chant the phrase as a unit: w Niemczech, na Węgrzech, we Włoszech. Three items, one breath, and the preposition is part of the phrase because each country has its own preferred one.

Which preposition for which country?

w for most countries (w Polsce, w Czechach, w Chinach, w Indiach). na for a fixed historical list that includes Węgry, Włochy, Ukrainę, Litwę, Łotwę, Białoruś, Słowację, Kubę, Sycylię — and also for regions and islands (na Śląsku, na Mazowszu, na Podhalu, na Helu). we / w before a word starting with w or f: we Włoszech, we Francji, we Wrocławiu. The preposition choice is a separate question from the ending, but since the two are learned together, this page keeps them together.

On na Ukrainie and w Ukrainie

Both are correct. The Rada Języka Polskiego stated on 25 July 2022 that either pairing is acceptable, and encouraged the w Ukrainie / do Ukrainy type — especially in official texts — because w is the preposition used with the names of independent states. The na Ukrainie form is older and deeply established in the tradition. You will not be wrong with either; you may be judged for the company you keep. This is a stylistic and political choice, not a grammatical error.

06

The nouns that break the rule

PWN lists exactly three groups of exceptions to the singular rules, and they are worth knowing precisely, because every Polish course tests them.

1. dom, pan, syn take -u

All three end in a hard consonant, so the rule predicts -e — and all three take -u anyway.

NounRule would giveActuallyExample
dom*domiedomuMieszkam w domu.
pan*paniepanuMówię o panu.
syn*syniesynuMyślę o synu.
Mind the collision with the vocative

panie is a real word — it is the vocative of pan, used when you address someone: Panie Marku! It is never the locative. So Mówię o panu (about Mr X) but Panie Marku, mówię o panu (Mr Mark, I am talking about you). The same shape-doubling happens with syn: the vocative is synu too, which is why this trio is so memorable.

2. dzień has two locatives

dzień accepts both (w) dniu and (we) dnie. They are not interchangeable in every context, though, because one of them is fossilised inside a fixed expression:

FormWhere it is used
w dniuthe normal modern form: w dniu wczorajszym, w dniu urodzin, and in officialese w dniu 28 października
we dniean obligatory part of we dnie i w nocy — “by day and by night”. This is the one place you must use it.

PWN’s wording is exact on this: the second form is an obligatory component of that expression. So we dnie i w nocy is correct and *w dniu i w nocy is not the idiom.

3. ręka has two locatives, both correct

This is a genuine free choice, not a mistake:

FormStatusExample
w ręcethe regular form (hard stem → -e)Mam klucz w ręce.
w rękuthe irregular form, equally correctTrzymam kubek w ręku.

You will hear w ręku more often in speech and w ręce more often in writing, but neither is an error. Note the contrast with the plural, which is regular: w rękach.

A fourth group worth adding, though PWN does not list it

Nouns in -anin (nationalities and inhabitants) lose the -in- in the plural, and that carries into the locative plural: mieszczanin → mieszczaninie in the singular, but mieszczanie → mieszczanach in the plural. The singular is perfectly regular (n is hard → -e); only the plural stem is shortened. The same pattern gives Amerykanin → Amerykanach, Rosjanin → Rosjanach.

Genuinely irregular plurals you will meet

These are not locative irregularities — they are irregular plural stems, and the -ach ending is still regular once you have the stem:

człowiek → ludziach · dziecko → dzieciach · oko → oczach · ucho → uszach · ręka → rękach · przyjaciel → przyjaciołach · ksiądz → księżach · książę → książętach.

07

The sound shifts — why the spelling changes

PWN calls the locative of hard-stem nouns the form “in which the greatest number of sound alternations occurs”. That is a warning worth taking seriously: the -e ending does not simply attach, it softens the final consonant, and Polish spelling records that softening. If you know the alternations, the spelling becomes predictable instead of arbitrary.

Vowel alternations

PWN explicitly notes that the -e ending triggers vowel changes in many words, not only consonant ones. These are the historical przegłos (ablaut) patterns, and they are the other half of the spelling difficulty:

The two mechanisms, side by side

The cleanest illustration of the whole section is one pair of words:

miasto → mieście: the st becomes ść (consonant shift) and the a becomes e (vowel shift). Both happen at once, because the ending -e forces the softening and the softening drags the vowel with it.

kocioł → kotle: the ł becomes l (consonant), the o disappears entirely (fleeting vowel), and the result has one syllable fewer than the nominative.

If you can see those two words as normal rather than bizarre, you have understood the section.

When the stem is already soft, nothing happens

This is the compensating good news. Nouns in the -u group do not soften, because they are already soft or hardened, so their locative is usually easy to predict from the nominative:

bank → banku · hotel → hotelu · koń → koniu · gość → gościu · liść → liściu · gracz → graczu · kraj → kraju · pokój → pokoju · muzeum → muzeum · centrum → centrum
The three groups in one line each

Hard stem → -e, and everything moves. miasto → mieście

k g ch l → -u, nothing moves. bank → banku · hotel → hotelu

Soft or hardened stem → -u, nothing moves. koń → koniu · gracz → graczu

This is why the notorious “hard” words — the ones with three sound changes in them — are all in the same group: they are the hard-stem -e words. Once you can tell hard from soft, you can predict both the ending and whether the spelling will surprise you.

08

Adjectives, pronouns and numerals

The good news about modifiers: the locative is one of the few places where Polish is simpler than you expect. Adjectives do not care whether the noun's stem is hard, soft or hardened. They care only about gender, and they have exactly four forms in the singular and two in the plural.

PWN writes the singular ending as -im ǁ -ym — two spellings of one ending. Where you write i and where you write y is pure Polish spelling: -im after a soft stem (tani → tanim, polski → polskim) and after k and g, because Polish spells the softened k and g with an i (wysoki → wysokim, drogi → drogim, długi → długim). Everywhere else it is -ym (dobry → dobrym, nowy → nowym, duży → dużym). The plural mirrors it exactly: -ich after soft stems and after k, g — -ych everywhere else.

The -em mistake

PWN singles out one error by name. Some speakers — under southern-Polish influence — extend the locative to *-em: *tamtem, *dobrem. These are wrong. The locative singular of an adjective is tanim, dobrym — never tamtem, dobrem.

There is exactly one legitimate home for such a form: geographical names used as nouns, where you will see w Kieleckiem, w Lubelskiem, w Poznańskiem (in the Kielce / Lublin / Poznań region). Those are nouns in their own right, not adjectives agreeing with anything.

Agreement in practice

Mieszkam w małym miasteczku. (neuter: -ym)
Rozmawiamy o nowej książce. (feminine: -ej)
Myślę o dobrym człowieku. (masculine: -ym)
Mówimy o polskich miastach. (plural: -ich)

Why there is an n- in nim, niej, nich

After a preposition, the third-person pronouns take an n-: o nim, o niej, o nich. Without a preposition there is no n-, which is why the genitive and accusative are go, jej, ich. Since the locative always has a preposition, the third person always shows the n- — the one case where you can rely on it without thinking.

Numerals: the -u rule

PWN is unusually blunt here: in the declension of numerals a single ending -u is used, without exception. Not -ym, not -ej, not -ach. A numeral in the locative simply ends in -u (spelled -u, -iu or -gu depending on the stem).

Ordinals are adjectives, not numerals

jeden is the exception that proves the rule — it is really an adjective, which is why it has jednym / jednej / jednych. And ordinals such as pierwszy, drugi, trzeci decline as adjectives, so they take the adjective endings: o pierwszym, o drugiej, o trzecich. That is what makes o trzeciej (at three o'clock) work — it is an ordinal feminine locative, not a numeral.

The five prepositions, one by one

Each of the five governs the locative in some meanings and something else in others. This is the section that separates people who “know the locative” from people who can use it.

w / we — inside, in

w + locative answers gdzie? — where something is. With the accusative it answers dokąd? — where something is going.

Locative — where?Accusative — where to?
Jestem w domu.Idę do domu.
Mieszkam w Warszawie.Jadę do Warszawy.
Jestem w sklepie.Idę do sklepu.
Pracuję w biurze.Idę do biura.

Notice that for “going to a place”, Polish normally uses do + genitive, not w + accusative. The w + accusative pattern appears when you cross into something: Wchodzę w wodę (I am walking into the water), Wsiadam w samochód.

we is the same preposition, inserted before a word that begins with w or f to make it pronounceable: we Włoszech, we Francji, we Wrocławiu, we wtorek.

na — on, at, in (a fixed list)

na + locative is the partner of na + accusative, and the same where/where-to logic applies:

Locative — where?Accusative — where to?
Jestem na poczcie.Idę na pocztę.
Książka leży na stole.Kładę książkę na stół.
Jestem na lotnisku.Jadę na lotnisko.
Mieszkam na Śląsku.Jadę na Śląsk.
Jestem na koncercie.Idę na koncert.

Which places take na rather than w is not a rule but a list, and it must be learned word by word. The main groups: open spaces and surfaces (na ulicy, na rynku, na plaży, na górze), islands and peninsulas (na Helu, na Kubie, na Sycylii), regions (na Śląsku, na Mazowszu, na Podhalu, na Mazurach), and a set of institutions and events (na poczcie, na lotnisku, na dworcu, na uniwersytecie, na koncercie, na weselu).

o — about

o + locative is the “about” preposition, and it is the one that makes the locative unavoidable in conversation:

mówić o pogodzie · myśleć o tobie · marzyć o wakacjach · rozmawiać o pracy · opowiadać o filmie · wiedzieć o wszystkim · zapomnieć o spotkaniu · pamiętać o urodzinach
Two os that take the accusative instead

pytać o (to ask about / for) and prosić o (to ask for) take the accusative: Pytam o film, Proszę o pomoc. And o + accusative also marks clock time and quantity: o trzeciej is locative, but o godzinę za wcześnie is accusative. The verb or the meaning decides, never the preposition alone.

po — around, over, after

po + locative means movement across or around an area, and it is the case of walking, wandering and travelling within a place:

chodzić po pokoju · spacerować po parku · jeździć po mieście · biegać po lesie · pływać po jeziorze · szukać po kątach
Three pos, three cases

po + locative = around an area (chodzę po parku). po + accusative = up to a limit, or go and fetch (po kolana — up to the knees; iść po chleb — to go for bread). po + genitive = after, in sequence (po obiedzie — after lunch; po pracy — after work). The locative is only the first of the three.

przy — by, next to, at

Physically beside something, and metaphorically “in the presence of” or “while occupied with”:

przy stole · przy oknie · przy drzwiach · przy komputerze · przy pracy · przy kolacji · przy okazji · obstawać przy swoim zdaniu

przy is one of the few prepositions that takes the locative and nothing else in modern Polish, so it is always safe: if you know the word after przy is a noun you decline, decline it as locative.

Verbs that demand the locative

This is the section to return to. Polish has a large set of verbs that require a fixed preposition plus the locative, and no rule generates them — you learn them as vocabulary, verb by verb. The compensation is that once you know the verb, the case is automatic: there is no choice to make.

VerbEnglishPatternExample
The five verbs learners get wrong most often

Myśleć o + Ms. — not accusative. Myślę o tobie.
Polegać na + Ms. — not w. Polegam na tobie.
Upierać się przy + Ms. — not na. Upieram się przy swoim.
Znać się na + Ms. — versus znać + accusative. Znam się na komputerach but Znam ten komputer.
Skupiać się na + Ms. Skupiam się na pracy.

Reflexive verbs: the się changes nothing

Many of these verbs are reflexive — koncentrować się, opierać się, upierać się, wzorować się, znać się. The się does not affect the case at all; it is part of the verb. Koncentruję się na zadaniu — same locative with or without the reflexive particle.

The traps

Trap 1 — Using -u after a soft consonant that is really hardened

The single most common error in this case is putting -i where -y belongs in feminine nouns: *pracy written as *praci. The hardened consonants c, cz, dz, dż, rz, sz, ż take -y. Say the word aloud: if the final consonant sounds hard, the ending letter is y.

NounWrongRightFinal consonant
praca*pracipracyc — hardened → -y
noc*nocinocyc → -y
twarz*twarzitwarzyrz → -y
plaża*plażyplażyż → -y; the only -y in the word is the ending
ulica*uliciulicyc → -y
moc*mocimocyc → -y

Trap 2 — dom, pan, syn

Hard stems, so the rule says -e; the language says -u. Three words, no logic, learn them. w domu, o panu, o synu. The -e forms *domie, *panie, *synie are wrong in the locative (panie exists, but as the vocative).

Trap 3 — Confusing locative with instrumental after z and nad

z + instrumental is “with” (z kolegą); z + genitive is “from” (z Polski). Neither is locative, and z never takes the locative at all.

The positional prepositions pod, nad, przed, za are the trap next door, and the reason the five-preposition rule needs a footnote. They take the instrumental for a static position (nad morzem, pod stołem, przed domem, za rogiem) and the accusative for movement towards (nad morze, pod stół, przed dom, za róg). They never take the locative — so the five-preposition list stays true, but “pod is instrumental” is only half the sentence. między works the same way (między nami for position, między okno a drzwi for direction).

Trap 4 — w versus na with places

Both are locative, so the case is not the problem — the preposition is. There is no rule; there is a list. The ones that surprise learners most: na poczcie, na dworcu, na lotnisku, na uniwersytecie, na koncercie, na weselu, na targu, na rynku, na ulicy, but w sklepie, w banku, w kinie, w teatrze, w restauracji, w muzeum, w szpitalu, w więzieniu.

A workable intuition

w tends to mean “inside an enclosed space”, na tends to mean “on a surface or in an open space”. That gets you w sklepie (inside) and na rynku (open square) right. It fails for institutions that historically were open marketplaces or gathering points — na poczcie, na uniwersytecie, na targu — which you simply have to learn.

Trap 5 — Forgetting the softening in -e words

Writing *miasto → miastoe or *las → lase is the beginner version; the intermediate version is writing *miaste (correct softening of st, but the vowel shift forgotten). miasto → mieście needs both: a → e and st → ść. When a word has an a before the final consonant, expect it to become e.

Trap 6 — The -ii spelling

Anglia → Anglii (two i), but ziemia → ziemi (one i). This is a spelling question rather than a grammatical one, and it is genuinely inconsistent across words. When a country or a word in -ia matters to you, check the dictionary once and then trust your memory of that word.

Trap 7 — -ach in the plural, not -ech

Only three words take -ech: Niemczech, Węgrzech, Włoszech. Everything else takes -ach, including the other plural countries (w Czechach, w Indiach, w Chinach). If you have learned the three as a chant, this trap is closed.

The four things that cause 90% of mistakes

1. Feminine hardened stems take -y, not -i.
2. dom, pan, syn take -u.
3. Only na, o, po, przy, w are locative prepositions.
4. Plural is -ach except Niemczech, Węgrzech, Włoszech.
