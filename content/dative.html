Celownik
the case of the one who receives

The case that answers komu? and czemu? — to whom?, to what?. It marks the receiver, the person something is done for or to, and the person an experience happens to.

Celownik is the third case in the Polish order, after mianownik and dopełniacz. Poles use it less often than the other cases, which is why its forms feel unfamiliar — and why a small, well-drilled set of rules covers almost all of it.

komu? czemu?
Its two questions — to whom? to what? The first for people, the second for everything else.
‑owi
The default masculine ending. Only a short closed list keeps ‑u.
‑e · ‑i · ‑y
Feminine singular — the last consonant of the stem decides which.
‑om
The plural, every gender, with no gender distinction at all.

01

What the case actually does

Six jobs cover nearly everything. Learn to recognise the job and the ending usually follows.

JOB 01

The receiver

Someone gets something. The thing is accusative, the person is dative.

Daję kotu mleko.I give the cat milk.
JOB 02

The beneficiary

An action is done for someone's sake, or at their expense.

Kupiłam mamie kwiaty.I bought mum flowers.
JOB 03

The loser

Something is taken, lost, or happens against someone's interest.

Ukradli mi rower.They stole my bike.
JOB 04

The experiencer

The person is not the subject — the thing is. The person feels it in the dative.

Podoba mi się Kraków.I like Kraków. (Kraków pleases to me.)
JOB 05

The possessor in trouble

Polish says the thing happened to the person, not to the object.

Zginął mi portfel.I lost my wallet. (The wallet was lost on me.)
JOB 06

The direction of an attitude

A handful of prepositions and adjectives point at a person or thing.

Dzięki tobie zdaliśmy.Thanks to you we passed.
The one-line test

If you can put to or for before the person in English, and the verb is not one of the accusative verbs, Polish wants the dative. Dałem to bratu. Kupiłem to mamie.

Masculine: ‑owi is the rule, ‑u is the exception

PWN is explicit: masculine nouns ending in a consonant take ‑owi almost always. A small, closed list keeps ‑u. Learn that list as vocabulary — everything else is safe with ‑owi.

The ł / l trap

ł is hard and takes ‑e: szkoła → szkole, cegła → cegle, perła → perle. l is soft and takes ‑i: sala → sali, fala → fali, rola → roli. One stroke of the pen changes the ending.

Feminine in ‑ia: watch the syllable

When the i is a syllable of its own, the dative doubles it: ‑ia → ‑ii. When it only softens the consonant before ‑a, you get a single ‑i.

‑ia → ‑ii

Maria → Marii armia → armii historia → historii linia → linii kolonia → kolonii

soft consonant + ‑a → ‑i

Ania → Ani Kasia → Kasi ciocia → cioci kuchnia → kuchni

Feminine with no ‑a

These take ‑i or ‑y, and the form is identical to the locative — the one place where two cases share a shape in the feminine.

noc → nocy rzecz → rzeczy twarz → twarzy radość → radości miłość → miłości jesień → jesieni kość → kości pamięć → pamięci krew → krwi sól → soli

Pronouns: long and short

This is the part that makes spoken Polish sound Polish. Most pronouns have two dative forms — a stressed one and a clitic one.

When to use which

Short form — the default

Unstressed, inside the sentence, after the verb.

Daj mi chwilę.Give me a moment.
Powiedz mu prawdę.Tell him the truth.

Long form — for weight

Emphasis, contrast, coordination, and always after a preposition.

Jemu daj, nie mnie.Give it to him, not to me.
Ku mnie szła.She was walking towards me.
Two rules that never bend

mi and ci cannot start a sentence. Mi się to podoba is wrong; say Podoba mi się to or Mnie się to podoba.

After every preposition, only the long form: ku mnie, ku tobie, ku niemu, dzięki mnie, przeciwko nim. Never ku mi or dzięki ci in that position.

Clitic order

When two pronouns meet, the dative comes first: Daję mu ją (I'm giving it to him), Powiem ci to, Kupię ci je. The reversed order Daję ją mu sounds wrong to a native ear.

The four verbs learners mix up

naprzeciw vs naprzeciwko

These two look like twins and take different cases. naprzeciw + dative: naprzeciw szkole. naprzeciwko + genitive: naprzeciwko szkoły. Saying naprzeciwko szkole is the classic mistake.

Not dative, despite appearances

mimo, pomimo, według, wedle, podług, wobec, dla and do all take the genitive. Według mnie — not według mi.

08

Special uses you will hear daily

Impersonal dative — the person as experiencer

Polish often puts the feeling in an adverb and the person in the dative. There is no subject at all.

Jest mi zimno.I am cold.
Jest mi przykro.I am sorry / I feel bad.
Wstyd mi.I am ashamed.
Żal mi.I feel sorry / I regret.
Szkoda mi.I feel sorry for / it's a shame.
Trzeba mi iść.I have to go.
Wolno mi.I am allowed.
Głupio mi.I feel silly.

Verbs with się that put the person in the dative

The grammatical subject is the thing; the person is the recipient of the experience. This is where English word order fails completely.

PolishLiterallyEnglish
Podoba mi się Kraków.Kraków pleases to me.I like Kraków.
Śniło mi się morze.The sea dreamt to me.I dreamt about the sea.
Udało mi się.It succeeded to me.I managed it.
Nie chce mi się.It doesn't want to me.I can't be bothered.
Wydaje mi się.It seems to me.It seems to me.
Przyszło mi do głowy.It came to my head.It occurred to me.
Nie mieści mi się w głowie.It doesn't fit in my head.I can't get my head around it.

Dative of loss — the Polish way of saying "my"

Instead of "my wallet disappeared", Polish says the wallet disappeared on me. The dative marks the person affected.

Zginął mi portfel.I lost my wallet.
Uciekła mi córka.My daughter ran away from me.
Zamarzły mi ręce.My hands froze.
Spaliło mi się ciasto.My cake burned.
Wypadł mi ząb.My tooth fell out.
Uciekł mi autobus.I missed the bus.

Ethical dative — pure attitude

A pronoun that adds no information, only involvement, impatience, or affection. Very colloquial, very Polish.

Idź mi stąd!Get out of here! (with feeling)
A ty mi tu nie hałasuj!And don't you make noise here!
Uważaj mi na siebie.You take care of yourself, you hear?

Adjectives that point at someone

These take a dative complement instead of do + genitive.

miły komu drogi komu bliski komu obcy komu znany komu wdzięczny komu wierny komu posłuszny komu oddany komu przychylny komu życzliwy komu przeciwny komu równy komu potrzebny komu przydatny komu niezbędny komu dostępny komu obojętny komu przyjazny komu wrogi komu
Jestem ci wdzięczny.I am grateful to you.
Ten film jest mi obcy.This film is alien to me.
To jest mi obojętne.It is all the same to me.
podobny is the exception

podobny takes do + genitive, not the dative: podobny do ojca. But bliski does take the dative: bliski ojcu.

Nouns and verbal nouns

Verbal nouns in ‑anie, ‑enie, ‑cie keep the verb's dative. Their non-verbal cousins often switch to dla + genitive — a genuine stylistic difference, noted by PWN.

The 4-step routine

1. Ask komu? czemu? — if the answer is a person receiving, you are in the dative. 2. Find the gender and the last stem consonant. 3. Apply the ending: ‑owi / ‑u / ‑e / ‑i / ‑y in the singular, ‑om in the plural. 4. If it is a pronoun, decide short or long — and never put the short one first.
