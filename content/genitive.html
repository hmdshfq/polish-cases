the case of “of”

The genitive answers kogo? czego? — “of whom? of what?”. It is the second most common case in Polish after the nominative, and it carries four jobs no other case shares: possession, quantity, the object of dozens of verbs, and the object of every negated verb. 01

What the genitive is

Polish marks the role of a noun with an ending, not with word order. Dopełniacz is the case that answers kogo? czego? — literally “of whom? of what?”. Its name comes from dopełniać, “to complete”: it completes another word — a noun, a verb, an adjective, a numeral — by naming the thing that word is about.

English “of” — and more

dom ojca

the house of the father → the father’s house

szklanka wody

a glass of water

pięć książek

five books — English has no “of” here, Polish still needs the genitive

The one that catches everyone

Mam samochód. → Nie mam samochodu.

I have a car. → I don’t have a car.

Widzę Annę. → Nie widzę Anny.

I see Anna. → I don’t see Anna.

Negation flips the accusative object into the genitive. This is not optional in standard Polish.

Why it matters

The genitive is where learners’ Polish either starts sounding native or stalls. Cases you can dodge with a different sentence; the genitive is unavoidable — it is in nie ma, in every number above four, in do, z, od, bez, dla, u, and it is the ending that turns “I don’t have time” from a mistake into a sentence.

You have already met 5 and 3. The genitive is the case that most often looks like the accusative — for masculine animates the two are identical — so that overlap is the one to watch.

02

The six jobs it does

Every genitive you will ever produce belongs to one of these six patterns. Recognise the pattern, and the ending follows far more easily than by guessing.

1 · Possession & belonging

samochód sąsiada

the neighbour’s car

córka mojej siostry

my sister’s daughter

stolica Polski

the capital of Poland

2 · Part of a whole

szklanka wody

a glass of water

kawałek chleba

a piece of bread

kilogram cukru

a kilo of sugar

3 · Quantity and number

pięć książek

five books

dużo pracy

a lot of work

kilka osób

a few people

4 · Object of certain verbs

Szukam mieszkania.

I’m looking for a flat.

Uczę się polskiego.

I’m learning Polish.

Boję się ciemności.

I’m afraid of the dark.

5 · Object of a negated verb

Nie mam czasu.

I don’t have time.

Nie znam tej pani.

I don’t know that lady.

Nie ma mleka.

There’s no milk.

6 · After prepositions

do sklepu, z Polski

to the shop, from Poland

bez cukru, dla dzieci

without sugar, for the children

według mnie

according to me

Two refinements worth knowing early

Genitive of time. Dates and clock-like time expressions stand in the genitive when they have a modifier: pierwszego maja, trzeciego stycznia, tego dnia, zeszłego roku, następnego tygodnia. Today’s date is a genitive: dwudziestego ósmego października.

Subjective vs objective genitive. śpiew ptaków — the birds sing (subject); obrona miasta — the city is defended (object). The same ending carries both directions; context decides, exactly as English “the shooting of the hunters” does.

Singular endings

Four endings, distributed by gender and by the final consonant of the stem. This table is the whole system in miniature.

-y or -i? The consonant decides

Feminine nouns (and masculine nouns in -a) take -i after soft stems and after k, g, l; -y after everything hard, including the “hardened” sounds c, cz, dz, dż, rz, sz, ż.

Good news

Neuter is the only gender with a single, exception-free genitive ending: -a. If you know a noun is neuter, you already know its genitive singular.

Masculine in -a: the “false feminine” group

Words like mężczyzna (man), kolega (colleague), poeta, kierowca (driver), starosta, tata are grammatically masculine but decline like feminines.

mężczyzna → mężczyzny · kolega → kolegi · kierowca → kierowcy · poeta → poety

04

-a or -u? The one real difficulty

For masculine inanimate nouns there is no rule that predicts the ending with certainty — the choice is lexical and partly historical. But it is not random either: PWN describes clear semantic tendencies, and a learner who knows them guesses right far more often than chance. Learn the tendencies, then memorise the frequent nouns.

-a tends to mean “a thing you can point at”

  • people and animals — studenta, psa, konia (exceptions: wołu, bawołu)
  • tools — noża, klucza, młotka
  • vessels — kielicha, czajnika, półmiska
  • measures, weights, numbers — hektara, grama, miliona, tuzina
  • months — stycznia, lutego, marca … grudnia
  • dances and games — walca, mazura, pokera
  • fruits, mushrooms — grapefruita, rydza, grzyba
  • car brands — mercedesa, fiata
  • body parts — nosa, palca, brzucha
  • many concrete everyday objects — chleba, sera, kotleta, komputera, telewizora, dworca

-u tends to mean “a mass, a process, an idea”

  • abstract nouns — czasu, strachu, bólu, spokoju, sensu, widoku, zachodu
  • substances and materials — cukru, ryżu, proszku, piasku, mleka (neuter), soku, oleju, gazu
  • collectives — tłumu, lasu, narodu
  • loanwords — biznesu, koncertu, makijażu, lakieru, uniwersytetu, hotelu
  • places and containers as spaces — sklepu, banku, parku, rynku, pociągu
  • verbal nouns in -anie/-enie are neuter, so they take -a: mieszkania, zdania

Note the trap in the last line: the “process” intuition is right, but those nouns are neuter and end in -a anyway.

Where the intuition breaks

komputer → komputera (loanword, but concrete object), telefon → telefonu (also a concrete object). chleb → chleba (a mass, but -a). ser → sera. wół → wołu and bawół → bawołu (animals, but -u). These are the ones to memorise, and the drills below do exactly that.

When the ending changes the meaning

Two nouns are famous for it, and examiners love them:

Practical strategy

Learn the genitive together with the nominative from the start — stół, stołu; sklep, sklepu — the way you learn gender. A noun card without its genitive is half a card. When you must guess: -u for anything abstract, foreign, or mass-like; -a for anything you could put on a table.

Plural endings

The plural genitive is the single most used form in Polish after the nominative — it appears after every number from five up. It has four outcomes: -ów, -y, -i, or no ending at all.

The fleeting -e-

Where the ending disappears, Polish often inserts -e- to keep the word pronounceable. It happens mostly after clusters of consonants:

Pattern to hold on to

Masculine leans towards -ów (except after soft and hardened consonants). Feminine and neuter lean towards no ending (except after soft consonants). If you must guess a masculine form, guess -ów.

06

Numbers, quantity, and the 2–4 trap

This is the single most useful application of the genitive plural, and the one learners get wrong most often — because Polish changes strategy at five.

NumberCase of the nounExample
1nominative singularjeden kot · jedna książka
2 – 4nominative plural (not genitive!)dwa koty · trzy książki · cztery okna
5 – 21genitive pluralpięć kotów · jedenaście książek · dwadzieścia lat
22 – 24nominative plural againdwadzieścia dwa koty · dwadzieścia trzy książki
25 and upgenitive pluraldwadzieścia pięć kotów · sto książek

The rule: the last element of the number decides. 12, 13, 14 behave like 5 — dwanaście lat, never “dwanaście lata”.

Masculine-personal nouns take a special numeral

When the noun is a man or a group including men, the numeral itself has a different genitive form, and even 2–4 switches to the genitive:

NumeralWith masculine-personal nounsTranslation
2dwóch studentówtwo students
3trzech studentówthree students
4czterech studentówfour students
5pięciu studentówfive students
10dziesięciu studentówten students

So “five students” is pięciu studentów, never “pięć studentów”. Compare pięć kotów — a cat is not masculine-personal.

Quantity words that always demand the genitive

dużo pracy mało czasu wiele osób kilka minut parę lat trochę mleka mnóstwo ludzi więcej pieniędzy mniej pracy pół godziny półtora roku półtorej godziny

półtora goes with masculine and neuter nouns, półtorej with feminine ones — both take the genitive singular.

Collective numerals

For mixed groups, children, and young animals Polish uses dwoje, troje, czworo, pięcioro + genitive plural:

dwoje dzieci · troje ludzi · pięcioro kurcząt

Their own genitive is -a: pięciorga dzieci — “of five children”.

Why “nie ma” loves numbers

Nie ma pięciu lat — “he is not five years old”, literally “there is no five years”. The numeral pięć itself becomes genitive pięciu because it is the object of nie ma. Two layers of genitive in three words.

The logic behind the list

PWN groups these verbs by meaning, and the grouping is genuinely predictive:

  • Negation-like meaning — brakować, odmawiać, zabraniać, zaprzestać, zaniechać, zapomnieć: each can be paraphrased with “not”. The genitive is the case of absence, so it fits.
  • Multi-stage or partial action — bronić, dochodzić, doczekać się, dostarczać, pilnować: you deal with part of a process, not a completed object.
  • Wish, demand, expectation — chcieć, pragnąć, żądać, wymagać, oczekiwać, życzyć: what you want is not yet “there”, so again the case of non-existence.

That is why chcę kawy (a part, some coffee) differs from chcę kawę (the whole cup, accusative). Both are correct; the genitive makes it partial.

Verbs that look genitive but are not

False friends from your instrumental and dative work

Dative: pomagać, dziękować, ufać, przyglądać się, przysłuchiwać się — Pomagam mamie, not “mamy”.
Instrumental: interesować się, zajmować się, brzydzić się, kierować, dowodzić, grozić, opiekować się, posługiwać się — Interesuję się historią, not “historii”.
Genitive but only with a preposition: cieszyć się z czegoś, śmiać się z czegoś — Cieszę się z sukcesu.

The four that look genitive and are not

Dative prepositions — a frequent mistake

dzięki (thanks to), wbrew (contrary to), naprzeciw (opposite), przeciw (against), ku (towards) all take the dative:

dzięki tobie · wbrew przepisom · naprzeciw oknu · ku niebu

“z” means two different cases

z + genitive = from, out of. z + instrumental = with, together with.

Wracam z pracy. — I’m coming back from work.

Kawa z mlekiem. — Coffee with milk.

Noun + noun: the prepositional alternative

Polish often prefers a bare genitive where English needs a phrase, but a prepositional version usually exists too:

klucz do samochodu ǁ klucz samochodu · okazja zobaczenia wystawy ǁ okazja do zobaczenia wystawy

09

Negation: the accusative becomes genitive

The rule is short and, in standard Polish, reliable: a negated verb that would take an accusative object takes a genitive object instead.

Affirmative (accusative)Negative (genitive)
Mam samochód.Nie mam samochodu.
Piję kawę.Nie piję kawy.
Czytam gazetę.Nie czytam gazety.
Mam dobry pomysł.Nie mam dobrego pomysłu.
Rozumiem to pytanie.Nie rozumiem tego pytania.
Znam tę panią.Nie znam tej pani.
Mam dwa koty.Nie mam dwóch kotów.

“nie ma” and “nie było”

Existence negated is always genitive — this is the construction you will use most in real life:

Nie ma mleka. · Nie ma problemu. · Nie ma nikogo. · Nie było czasu. · Nie będzie egzaminu.

When the rule does not apply

  • The verb already governs another case. Pomagam mamie → Nie pomagam mamie (dative stays). Interesuję się historią → Nie interesuję się historią (instrumental stays).
  • “być” with a predicate noun. Jestem nauczycielem → Nie jestem nauczycielem (instrumental stays).
  • “to jest / to nie jest”. To nie jest mój samochód — nominative, because there is no accusative object at all.
  • The object sits behind a preposition. Mówię o tobie → Nie mówię o tobie (locative stays).
  • The negated word is the subject, not the object. Nikt nie przyszedł — nominative subject.
Register

In casual speech you will hear nie mam czas or nie widzę Annę. It is common, it is understood, and in an exam or a job interview it is wrong. Standard Polish requires the genitive, and natives notice its absence.

Pronouns under negation

The object pronoun also switches: Mam go → Nie mam go; Widzę ją → Nie widzę jej; Znam to → Nie znam tego.

Masculine go looks identical because the accusative and genitive of on coincide. Feminine and neuter show the change clearly.

The three questions to ask before any noun

1. Is the verb negated? → genitive. 2. Is there a number of 5+ or a quantity word? → genitive plural. 3. Is there a preposition from the genitive list? → genitive. If none of the three, the noun is probably not genitive at all.

The four rules that cover most real sentences

  1. Negated verb → accusative becomes genitive. Nie mam czasu.
  2. 5 and above, and quantity words → genitive plural. pięć książek, dużo pracy.
  3. Genitive prepositions → do, od, z, u, bez, dla, oprócz, koło, około, obok, podczas, według, wobec, zamiast, pomimo, w ciągu, z powodu.
  4. Verb + genitive → szukać, uczyć się, bać się, potrzebować, używać, słuchać, pilnować, wymagać, oczekiwać, życzyć, brakuje.
